Some relationships are full of words.

Some are built around everything except words.

A ride to the airport. A repaired sink. Money quietly transferred when you were short. A question about your tires that meant *are you okay?* A meal placed in front of you without asking. A joke instead of an apology. A nod instead of *I am proud of you.*

For many people, fathers live somewhere in that territory: care expressed through action, conflict handled through silence, love present but rarely named.

Not every father relationship is like this. Some are openly affectionate. Some are estranged. Some are dangerous. Some barely existed. Some contain more pain than love.

That is why “things I never said to my dad” is not one letter.

It is a category of unfinished sentences.

## maybe you wanted him to be proud of you

This is one of the recurring themes in public letters to fathers.

Not simply *I wanted your approval.*

Something more complicated:

*I built a whole life around not needing your approval, and I still noticed when you gave it.*

You may have wanted to hear:

- I see how hard you worked.
- I like the person you became.
- I was wrong about you.
- You do not have to prove anything to me.
- I am proud of you.

If those words never came, it can feel embarrassing to admit they still matter.

Write that embarrassment too.

> I wish I had stopped pretending your opinion meant nothing to me.

That can be the whole beginning.

## maybe you were proud of him

In a grief-support thread asking what people wished they had said before a parent died, one answer was painfully simple: they wished they had told their dad they were proud of him.

Parents are usually positioned as the people who give approval.

But children see parents struggle, change, survive, fail, start over, get sober, work impossible jobs, care for relatives, learn tenderness.

Sometimes the unsaid sentence is:

> I saw what it cost you.

Or:

> You did better than what was done to you.

Or simply:

> I was proud to be your kid.

## maybe the relationship was built around distance

An unsent letter to a father described growing up knowing there was love while also feeling opposed by him, then moving away, barely keeping in touch, and only later understanding some of the conflict differently.

Distance can start as independence.

Then years pass.

Neither person knows how to re-enter.

Calls get shorter. You talk about weather, work, repairs, appointments. The deeper conversation becomes harder precisely because you postponed it so long.

If that is your relationship, do not begin with a perfect declaration.

Begin in the language you actually shared.

> I know we never talked like this.

That sentence opens a door without pretending the door was always open.

## maybe you are angry with him

Death, age or family loyalty do not cancel anger.

You can love your father and still be angry that he was absent, frightened you, mocked things that mattered to you, never apologized, chose work over family, treated somebody badly, demanded closeness he did not build, or expected you to understand him without trying to understand you.

You do not have to turn the anger into forgiveness for the letter to be valid.

Try being concrete.

Instead of:

> You ruined my childhood.

you might write:

> I learned to listen to the sound of your key in the door because I needed to know what kind of night it was going to be.

Specific memories carry their own weight.

## maybe you understand him more now and still do not excuse him

Adulthood can make parents more legible.

You learn what money costs. How exhaustion changes a person. How little preparation people get before raising children. You learn family history they kept from you.

Understanding can arrive.

But understanding is not a court order to forgive.

You can write:

> I understand why you were that way now. I also wish you had been different.

Two truths can stand beside each other.

## maybe love was said sideways

Think about the phrases that meant more than their literal meaning.

“Text me when you get there.”

“Check your oil.”

“Do you need anything from the store?”

“Take some food with you.”

Maybe *I love you* was rare. Maybe it was constant.

Write down the dialect your relationship used.

Then translate it if you want.

> When you stayed outside in the cold fixing my car, I knew what you were saying even though neither of us said it.

## if he is alive, decide whether the words need him

An unsent letter is useful even when the recipient is alive.

Write the full version first. Then ask whether any part of it should become a conversation.

You do not have to send the whole emotional archive.

Maybe the sentence you actually say is:

> I realized I never told you I am proud of you.

Or:

> There is something from when I was younger that I still want to talk about.

Or:

> I know we are not a family that says this much, but I love you.

If contact is unsafe or unwanted, the answer may be none of it.

A person being your parent does not entitle them to access to you.

## if he is gone

People grieving fathers often mention the things they wish they had preserved: voicemails, recipes, answers to practical questions, cards, photographs, the sound of a laugh.

One person wrote about missing their dad's cooking and wishing they had written down answers to food questions they used to call him about.

That kind of loss is almost unbearably ordinary.

If your dad is dead, the letter can contain what you would tell him now: what happened after he died, what you finally understand, what you are still angry about, what you wish you had asked, what his grandchildren are like, what you caught yourself doing exactly like him.

You are not required to write a goodbye.

## prompts for the words that got stuck

- Dad, I never told you that…
- I wanted you to notice…
- I wish you had asked me…
- I was scared of you when…
- I understand now why…
- I still do not understand why…
- I was proud of you when…
- The thing you taught me without trying was…
- The ordinary thing I miss is…
- I became like you in this one way…
- I spent years trying not to become like you in this other way…
- If we had one honest hour, I would tell you…

One true answer is better than a complete worksheet.

## not every father letter ends with love

Some end with anger. Some with boundaries. Some with grief. Some with gratitude. Some with nothing resolved.

The point is not to write the father relationship you think you should have had.

It is to write the one that actually existed.

If there is a sentence for your dad that has never had a place to land, **things unsaid** on the wall can hold it without requiring a reply.
