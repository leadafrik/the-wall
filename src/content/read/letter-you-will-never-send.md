An unsent letter is useful because it separates two decisions people often collapse into one:

**Do I need to say this?**

and:

**Does this person need to receive it?**

Those are different questions.

You can have words that are real, urgent and overdue without deciding that delivering them would be kind, safe or useful.

People write unsent letters to exes, parents, dead relatives, former friends, people who hurt them, people they hurt, and people they still love but should not contact.

Public unsent-letter communities are full of contradictions: apology without a request for forgiveness, love without a request to return, anger next to tenderness, and people acknowledging that contact might reopen something the recipient has already closed.

The letter gives those contradictions somewhere to coexist.

## begin with the sentence you keep rehearsing

Do not begin with background.

Begin with pressure.

- I wish you knew that…
- I am still angry about…
- I understand something now that I did not understand then.
- I miss you, but I do not want you back.
- I am sorry for…
- The thing I never told you was…

The beginning does not need to be fair yet.

It needs to be true enough to get you into the letter.

## write to the person, not the courtroom

A common trap is writing as though an invisible jury will decide whose version of events is correct.

You start proving dates, explaining context, anticipating objections.

Try writing what happened **to you**, in your interior language.

Not:

> You objectively ruined everything.

But:

> After that, I stopped trusting what you said. I still notice that in myself.

Specific feeling is often more honest than total judgment.

## include the part that makes your story less clean

Deep unsent letters often turn when the writer stops protecting a preferred version of themselves.

Maybe:

- you were hurt and you also retaliated;
- you were young, but you still caused harm;
- you miss someone who treated you badly;
- you are grateful for parts of a relationship you would never return to;
- you want an apology and know it may not change anything;
- you are sorry and still believe ending the relationship was right.

One recent r/UnsentLetters apology separated explanation from excuse: personal struggles might explain behavior but did not make it acceptable. Another apology thread prompted disagreement over whether contacting the recipient would provide closure or disturb peace already built.

That disagreement is why writing and sending should be separate decisions.

## if you are apologizing, remove the hidden request

An apology becomes complicated when it quietly asks the recipient to rescue the writer from guilt.

Watch for:

- Please tell me you do not hate me.
- I need you to know I am different now.
- I cannot forgive myself unless you forgive me.

You may feel those things. Put them in the unsent version if they are true.

But if you later send an apology, the recipient should not have to comfort you for having hurt them.

A cleaner apology contains:

1. what I did;
2. what I understand about its effect;
3. what I should have done differently;
4. what responsibility looks like now.

Forgiveness is not an item you can require.

## if you are angry, do not sanitize the first draft

Write the accusation.

Write the insult if it is what is in your head.

Write the unfair sentence.

Then do not send that draft.

The freedom of an unsent letter is that it can hold language that would be needlessly destructive in a delivered message.

You are allowed to discover the feeling before deciding what deserves to become communication.

## if you miss them, do not turn missing into a command

Missing somebody does not mean you should contact them.

You can write:

> I miss our Sunday mornings.

without concluding:

> therefore we should try again.

You can let longing be evidence of attachment rather than evidence of a plan.

## ask who the letter is for

Halfway through, ask:

**If they never read this, what still matters about writing it?**

Maybe:

- I want a record of what I finally understand.
- I need to say the full thing once.
- I want to stop rehearsing it in fragments.
- I want to admit what I did.
- I want to address somebody who is dead.

If your only answer is *I need them to respond a certain way*, the real need may still sit outside the letter.

That is useful information too.

## should you ever send it?

Maybe.

But not while the letter is still hot.

Ask:

### would contact violate a boundary?

If they asked for no contact, an eloquent letter is still contact.

### could sending create risk?

Especially where there has been abuse, stalking, legal conflict or threats.

### is the letter for them, or for your relief?

Relief is valid. It does not automatically create an obligation for another person to receive what relieves you.

### are you prepared for silence?

No reply is a possible reply.

### are you prepared for a response you dislike?

They may disagree. They may be angry. They may have moved on.

## what to do when it is done

Keep it. Delete it. Print it. Destroy it. Rewrite a shorter version. Use one sentence as the start of a real conversation. Leave it somewhere anonymous.

Do not force symbolic closure if you do not feel closed.

Evidence on expressive writing is mixed. It should not be sold as a guaranteed therapeutic intervention. Its simpler value may be enough: it makes a complete piece of language out of something that had been repeating in fragments.

## a structure if you are stuck

**1. what I never said**  
One direct sentence.

**2. what happened from where I stood**  
No biography. The part that still has charge.

**3. what I understand now**  
About them, yourself or the situation.

**4. what is still unresolved**  
Do not manufacture peace.

**5. what I am leaving here**  
Maybe only: *I am done writing for tonight.*

## a letter can be real without being sent

Some recipients are dead. Some are unsafe. Some relationships have ended so completely that delivery would only reopen them. Sometimes the recipient is your younger self.

The absence of delivery does not make the address meaningless.

If yours needs somewhere to exist without becoming a message in their inbox, **things unsaid** on the wall is one place it can go.
