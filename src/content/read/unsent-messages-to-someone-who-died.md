Sometimes grief reaches for the phone.

Not because you have forgotten.

Because for a fraction of a second the old route still exists in your body:

*I should tell them this.*

Then the fact of death arrives again.

People write to the dead in text drafts, journals, notes apps, letters, grave-site cards, social posts and private documents.

The messages are often startlingly ordinary.

*You would hate what they did to the kitchen.*

*I finally got the job.*

*Your team won.*

*I forgot how you made the sauce.*

*I miss you.*

Writing to someone who died is not necessarily an attempt to deny death.

Sometimes it is what happens when a relationship has ended physically but language still has momentum.

## the message can be about today

You do not need to write a eulogy.

Tell them the thing you would actually have called about.

A family update. A stupid joke. Something at work. A new person in the family. A problem you would have asked them to solve.

One grief-support poster wrote a birthday message to a father that moved through family updates, fear about the poster's mother, missing the father's cooking, and wishing they had written down answers to questions they used to call him about.

That shape feels true because grief rarely stays in one register.

Love and logistics sit next to each other.

## write the questions too

Some questions become permanently unanswered after someone dies.

Were you proud of me?

Why did you keep that secret?

What was the recipe?

What were you afraid of?

Why did you make that choice?

Do not rush to answer for them.

An unanswered question can remain an unanswered question on the page.

## you can be angry with someone who died

Death does not make every relationship uncomplicated.

People in grief communities write about being furious that someone died, angry about the practical mess left behind, angry about old harm, angry about choices made before death.

There is social pressure to become reverent after somebody dies.

You do not have to obey it in private writing.

> I love you and I am angry with you.

Both can be true.

## if you did not get a final conversation

Many people do not.

Death may be sudden. The person may lose consciousness. You may live far away. The relationship may be estranged. You may simply assume there will be another week.

A recent grief-support discussion from someone whose mother was in hospice drew responses from people who wished they had asked more questions, saved more voice, or had one more conversation. Another commenter described looking at unsent messages to a mother after sudden illness and death.

There is no way to retroactively create the final conversation you should have had.

But you can write the conversation you did not get.

Not as a substitute for reality.

As a record of what remained.

## keep the voice informal if that is how you talked

If your messages were usually:

> call me when u can

you do not need to become a poet because the person died.

Write:

> you will not believe what happened

or:

> I need your opinion

or:

> this would make you laugh

A message can sound like the relationship sounded.

Ordinary voice may bring the person closer to memory than formal memorial language does.

## what should you do with the messages?

Whatever is least performative for you.

Keep them private. Write letters. Put them with photographs. Share selected messages with family. Leave one somewhere anonymous.

There is no need to choose one method forever.

## is it “healthy” to write to someone who died?

That question comes up because people worry there is a correct way to accept death.

Modern grief research includes **continuing bonds**: the relationship with someone who died can continue internally through memory, identity, ritual, values and imagined connection.

That is an established area of bereavement research.

But it should not be simplified into “writing to the dead is good for you.”

Studies distinguish different forms of continuing bonds and their relationships with grief and adjustment. People vary. Writing can feel meaningful to one person and unbearable to another.

The safer truth is:

**many bereaved people continue to address, remember and feel connected to people who died. You are not required either to do this or stop doing it on somebody else's schedule.**

## if writing makes the pain sharper

Stop.

You can come back or never come back.

Grief writing is not an assignment.

If you are struggling to function, feel unsafe, or are thinking about harming yourself, use real human support rather than relying on a writing exercise. In the U.S., call/text/chat 988. In the UK and Ireland, Samaritans is free at 116 123. Find A Helpline lists support by country.

## message starters

- You would have laughed at…
- I wish I could ask you…
- Something happened today and my first thought was…
- I found one of your…
- I am angry that…
- I finally understand…
- I still do not understand…
- I wish I had told you…
- The thing I remember most clearly today is…
- I made it through…
- I wish you were here for…

Write as little or as much as comes.

## no final message is required

You do not have to write goodbye.

The message can end with:

> anyway. I just wanted to tell you.

That is how millions of ordinary conversations end.
